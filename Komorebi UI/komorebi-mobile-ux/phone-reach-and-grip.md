# Phone reach and grip

Phone ergonomics depends on the hand-device configuration.

## Grip varies

Field observation and controlled research both support the practical conclusion that people do not hold phones in one universal way.

Relevant modes include:

- one-handed thumb use;
- cradle grip with the other hand interacting;
- two-handed / two-thumb use;
- temporary grip changes during a flow.

Do not treat field-observation percentages as universal population constants. Their strongest use is proving **grip diversity**.

## Functional thumb area

Research models the area the thumb can reach while the hand continues gripping the device. That area varies with:

- device size;
- hand size;
- grip geometry;
- finger support behind the device.

So the useful question is not “is this inside the thumb zone?” but:

**Is this action inside a comfortable functional area for the configuration that matters?**

## Direction matters

Motor cost is not radial. Equal distance in different directions can require different thumb posture and muscle effort.

Avoid simplistic concentric-circle reach models.

## Preferred vs non-preferred hand

Controlled studies show measurable differences in speed, accuracy and perceived ease between hands.

If one-handed operation matters, test both left and right hands rather than mirroring conclusions from the designer's preferred hand.

## Larger phones

As screens grow, reachable screen area does not grow in proportion to the display. Tall top corners become more expensive without the hand itself growing.

Possible responses include:

- moving repeated actions lower;
- contextual actions near the object;
- gestures that complement visible controls;
- reachability mechanisms;
- allowing comfortable two-handed use.

## Edges: accuracy vs comfort

Research has found edge targets can be accurate while center targets feel easier or more comfortable.

Do not collapse these into one metric.

Measure separately when relevant:

- reachability;
- comfort;
- accuracy;
- speed;
- regrip;
- accidental activation.

## High-frequency actions

Repeated actions benefit from:

- stable location;
- short travel;
- sufficient hit region;
- minimal regrip;
- immediate feedback.

The 50th repetition matters more than the first impression.

## Destructive and rare actions

Easy reach is not automatically desirable. Rare destructive actions can tolerate more deliberate movement if they remain discoverable and accessible.

Motor friction can sometimes protect against accidental activation; it should never become arbitrary obscurity.
